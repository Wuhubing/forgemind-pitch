[~45s] Text-to-CAD tools today generate geometry. It looks plausible, but machines fail in ways a render can’t show: parts interfere, joints lock, a gearbox has the right ratio but tips over. A real example from our own system: we asked a quadruped robot to stand still. The pose metric reported a 2.1-degree tilt, so it returned PASS. When our vision model watched the simulation frames, the robot had tipped onto its side. The numbers were wrong and the video wasn’t. Our conclusion: the evaluator has to watch the machine actually work.

[~40s] That’s what AutoMech does. You type a goal, for example “design a 4-to-1 planetary reducer.” The system writes a parametric CAD script and builds the parts and joints. You get an orbitable 3D model, a physics video with metrics, and, if it fails, the cause and how to fix it. 04

[~50s] Under the hood is a closed loop. Design: the model writes one CAD script. Build: parts, joints and meshes. Check: interference, support and collision. Judge: a vision model inspects six views for structural faults. Simulate: we drive the input and measure the output. Refine: every failure becomes concrete repair evidence for the next design. Under strict review this loop has already produced two working mechanisms: a four-planet reducer that measures 4.0 to 1 with every planet orbiting and spinning, and a slider-crank with a 24-millimeter stroke and stable forward and return motion.

05

[~45s] Why is this defensible? Text-to-CAD tools stop at geometry; we test behavior. Three things. First, executable dependencies: critical dimensions share parameters and formulas instead of hard-coded coordinates. In a one-task pilot on a three-planet reducer, that was 100 out of 100 versus 25 out of 100. It’s a small sample and we’re scaling it up. Second, a task-level evaluator that tests what the machine does, not how it looks. Third, a failure-data flywheel: every failure gives us collision points, trajectories, video and the fix that worked. That’s data geometry-only tools never collect.
